I am a master's student at Johns Hopkins University, studying fluid mechanics and computational fluid dynamics under the guidance of Prof. Rajat Mittal. My work involves applying numerical methods and simulations to understand a range of complex flow problems, including biofluid dynamics and other flow conditions.

I obtained my Bachelor’s degree in Theoretical and Applied Mechanics at the Southern University of Science and Technology (SUSTech), where I conducted research in turbulence modeling and large-eddy simulation in Prof. Lian-ping Wang's group, laying a solid foundation in both theoretical and numerical methods.

My research interests include computational fluid dynamics, turbulence modeling, biofluids, multiphase flows, and high-performance computing. I am focused on advancing simulation techniques to better understand complex fluid behavior and address various fluid mechanics challenges.

Computational modeling of bariatric surgery and multiphase flow simulation

Currently, I am working with Prof. Rajat Mittal at the Flow Physics and Computational Lab (FPCL) to employ computational fluid dynamics to investigate how laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG), a weight-loss surgery that reduces stomach size, affects gastric functions. Using the in-house solver ViCar3D with immersed boundary and volume-of-fluid methods, the research developed post-operative stomach models with different volumes and motility patterns to simulate various surgical outcomes. Through multiphase flow simulations, the study revealed that reduced stomach volume accelerates gastric emptying while impaired motility decreases mixing efficiency. The research uncovered key mechanisms driving these changes, including altered intragastric pressure distributions and modified antral contraction patterns. This work has been presented at the 2024 APS Division of Fluid Dynamics, and a manuscript based on this study has been submitted to Journal of Biomechanical Engineering.

Large-eddy Simulation with Wall Model in Lattice Boltzmann Method for Turbulent Channel Flow

For my undergraduate thesis project, I focused on developing an efficient computational framework for simulating turbulent flows by combining Large-eddy Simulation (LES) with the Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM). By implementing the Smagorinsky subgrid-scale model and Musker wall model into the in-house direct numerical simulation code in our group, the study achieved accurate turbulent channel flow simulations with half the grid resolution and double the time step length compared to traditional methods. The developed computational modules have enabled subsequent research on complex urban canopy flows. This work was supported by the National Innovation and Entrepreneurship Training Program and recognized with an Excellent Undergraduate Thesis Award.

Computational Modeling of Marine Ecosystems: Phytoplankton Thermal Diversity Research

During my visiting year at MIT, I collaborated with Prof. Mick Follows to investigate the relationship between thermal diversity and ecological niches in marine phytoplankton through computational modeling and data analysis. The study examined how intraspecific thermal diversity in coccolithophores, particularly Gephyrocapsa huxleyi strains, influences their global distribution patterns. Utilizing the Darwin ecosystem model, the research analyzed multi-dimensional data including cell size, thermal type, biomass, temperature, time, and location variables. Through statistical analysis, we demonstrated how strains with different thermal adaptations distributed across distinct oceanic regions, contributing to global biogeographic patterns. A manuscript based on this work has been submitted to Ecology Letters and has completed its second round of revisions.
